Learning outcomes

Module code: SSDC_PCOM7E

This page maps my module activities and artefacts to the formal learning outcomes of SSDC_PCOM7E (Secure Software Development). The evidence presented across the units demonstrates the development of secure SDLC awareness, secure design reasoning, vulnerability analysis, testing discipline, implementation practice, and reflective professional growth.

Learning Outcome 1

Identify and manage security risks as part of a software development project

This learning outcome is evidenced most clearly in Units 1, 3, 4, 5, 10 and 11. In Unit 1, I examined secure software development from the perspective of lifecycle integration, risk-aware culture and the importance of embedding security early rather than treating it as a final-stage control. This established the principle that secure development depends on process, governance and review discipline as much as on code.

In Units 3 and 4, this risk perspective became more technical. The comparison between unmanaged and managed languages clarified how programming language characteristics shape vulnerability exposure, while the study of regular expression denial of service showed that even apparently minor implementation decisions can create meaningful availability risks. Unit 5 reinforced this further by linking testing strategies to attack surface reduction, showing that boundary analysis, equivalence partitioning and structured test planning can help identify security weaknesses before deployment.

In Units 10 and 11, risk analysis widened again to distributed and emerging environments. These units highlighted the security implications of distributed architectures, virtualised systems, encryption in transit, and future-facing technologies such as IoT, fog computing and cyber-physical systems. Across the module, I therefore demonstrated the ability to identify risks not only at code level, but also at architectural and operational levels.

Learning Outcome 2

Critically analyse development problems and determine appropriate methodologies, tools and techniques to solve them

This outcome is evidenced across Units 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 9 and 10. Unit 1 introduced the contrast between predictive and adaptive SDLC approaches, prompting critical consideration of how security must be integrated differently in structured and iterative development contexts. Unit 2 extended this by focusing on UML modelling and secure system planning, showing how modelling techniques support clearer reasoning about weaknesses, system behaviour and mitigation paths.

Units 4, 5 and 6 deepened this analytical approach by moving into implementation-level techniques. In Unit 4, regular expressions and recursion were examined not simply as programming concepts, but as design choices with security implications. In Unit 5, the study of testing terminology, standards and methods strengthened my ability to determine which forms of testing were most appropriate for different security concerns. Unit 6 then introduced linters and Python quality tools, demonstrating how automated analysis can support secure and consistent code development.

In Unit 9, these analytical and technical skills were applied more directly through API development, ontology design and structured validation. In Unit 10, the focus shifted to broader architectural patterns, requiring critical comparison of distributed, virtualised and service-oriented approaches. Across these activities, I demonstrated not just the use of tools and methods, but the ability to justify why certain approaches were appropriate in relation to risk, context and system design.

Learning Outcome 3

Design, develop and adapt programs and produce a solution that meets the design brief and critically evaluate the solutions produced

This learning outcome is evidenced especially in Units 2, 5, 6, 8 and 9, as well as in the broader implementation artefacts within the portfolio. Unit 2 involved the use of UML-based modelling to support secure design reasoning, including the representation of weaknesses and mitigation logic through structured diagrams. This contributed to design clarity before implementation.

Units 5 and 6 strengthened the development and evaluation dimension by focusing on testing, linting and code quality. These activities reinforced that secure software is not only about producing functionality, but also about ensuring correctness, consistency and resistance to misuse. Unit 8 added a practical cryptography element, demonstrating the use of cryptographic concepts in code while also recognising the limitations of simplified or classical examples when compared with real-world secure systems.

Unit 9 provided the clearest direct evidence for this learning outcome through the design and implementation of a secure CRUD API in Flask. This required structured input validation, predictable endpoint behaviour, automated testing and design reflection. The solution was not only implemented, but also critically evaluated in relation to limitations such as in-memory persistence, lightweight access control and the need for stronger production-grade security mechanisms. This reflects both practical development competence and evaluative judgement.

Learning Outcome 4

Systematically develop and implement the skills required to be an effective member of a development team in a virtual professional environment, adopting real-life perspectives on team roles and organisation

This learning outcome is evidenced through the collaborative discussions, peer responses, reflective work and professional development material included in the portfolio. Unit 1 began this process through the emphasis on team project structure, role awareness and the need for secure development to be supported by communication and shared responsibility.

The collaborative discussion activities required constructive academic engagement with the work of peers, including critique, clarification and the refinement of security reasoning. These exchanges demonstrated not only technical understanding but also the ability to work responsibly within a shared learning environment. The reflective writing developed across Units 3 and 4 further supported this outcome by showing how my thinking evolved from a primarily technical interpretation of software security towards a broader view that includes governance, architecture, review discipline and system behaviour under misuse conditions.

The inclusion of a professional skills matrix and development action plan also supports this outcome by translating module learning into forward-looking professional development goals. This demonstrates that the module has contributed not only to technical capability, but also to reflective practice and the continued development of secure professional judgement.
